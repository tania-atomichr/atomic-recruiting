# atomic✳HR voice for role pages

## Who's talking
A thoughtful hiring manager explaining a role to a capable person over coffee. Not an ad, not a policy document, not a template. Clarity is the first rule: a reader with no context understands what the company does and what the role is on one read. When any other rule fights clarity, clarity wins.

Aim for: candidate-first, candid, respectful, practical, ambitious without hype, startup-appropriate without glorifying disorder. Warm and specific, with restraint.

## Mechanics
- Second person ("you") for the candidate. "We" for the hiring team when it reads naturally.
- **Contractions are the default** (you'll, it's, don't, we're). "You will not / does not" reads stiff.
- Short paragraphs. Mix sentence lengths naturally. One main idea per sentence.
- Plain copulas ("is", "are"). Don't dance around them.
- Concrete verbs and nouns. Keep the plain professional word; don't upgrade it to a snappier synonym (spot for identify, ship for release, docs for documentation). That swap is what makes copy read fake.
- Headings carry meaning ("Give each business clarity, and the group a complete picture"), not category labels ("About the role").
- **No em dashes (—), no en dashes (–), no semicolons.** Use commas, periods, parentheses, or "and". Ranges are written "$6,000 to $8,000 USD per month" and "30 to 45 minutes".

## Describe evidence, not personality
- Good: "When a number doesn't reconcile, you trace the cause and close the loop with the owner."
- Weak: "You're a problem solver."

## State hard things as conditions to weigh, not warnings
- Good: "The process differs across the three companies, so the first phase is mapping and prioritizing."
- Avoid: "You must be comfortable with chaos."
- Good: "Calendar accuracy matters because the role coordinates across several time zones."
- Avoid: stories about someone being let go for a mistake.

## Banned patterns (scan every draft)
- Em dashes, en dashes, semicolons
- Hype and filler: "fast-paced environment", "rock star", "ninja", "wear many hats", "world-class", "cutting-edge", "exciting opportunity", "passionate", "dynamic", "high impact" used for ordinary work
- "Not X, it's Y" / "This isn't just X" pivots, and aphoristic one-line morals
- Rule-of-three flourishes (three stacked adjectives or parallel phrases for rhythm)
- Negative parallelism ("no X, no Y, no Z")
- Mirrored opening and closing lines, slogan cadence
- Vague attribution ("studies show", "many believe") and overgeneralization
- Stacked hedging ("may potentially help to")
- Abstract appreciation nouns and filler abstractions ("a testament to", "synergy", "excellence")
- Explicit signposting ("In this section", "Let's dive in", "It's worth noting")
- Long sentences carrying several unrelated ideas
- Unexplained internal jargon
- Claims that every responsibility is equally strategic

## Read-aloud test
Read the hero and the application close out loud. If either sounds like an ad or a LinkedIn post, rewrite it until it sounds like a person talking.
