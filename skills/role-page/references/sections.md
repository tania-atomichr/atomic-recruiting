# The twelve sections

Each section lists what it must answer, what to write, the quality test, and what to avoid. Counts are targets when the evidence supports them (see SKILL.md), never quotas to fill.

## 1 Hero

**Must answer:** What result, problem, or mandate defines this role? Why would the right candidate care right away? What are the key decision facts?

**Write:**
- Role title and department as short metadata
- A hook of about 5 to 12 words
- A two-sentence summary of the mandate and context
- Three key facts, usually location, setup, and compensation
- A one-line mission statement for the summary panel
- Quick details: reporting line, seniority, schedule, pay cadence, time off (when known)

**Quality test:** a candidate understands the role's purpose and basic terms without scrolling. The right one thinks "this is the problem I know how to solve."

**Avoid:** the role title as the headline. Generic culture promises. Company history. Compensation hidden further down. A summary that repeats the headline.

## 2 Why this role exists

**Must answer:** What changed or hit a limit? Why hire now? What will this person make possible? What's hard about the mandate?

**Write:**
- A specific heading that states the situation
- A one-sentence lead that sharpens the business need
- Two to four short paragraphs: context, current condition, mandate, opportunity
- One central challenge that names the most important tradeoff
- Two or three "why now" or "what this hire makes possible" cards, each with a different reason

**Quality test:** the business need is clear, and the role doesn't read like a punishment for problems that piled up.

**Avoid:** opening with "We are looking for". Repeating the responsibilities. Treating normal growth as a crisis. Vague lines like "the company is scaling rapidly".

## 3 What you'll be trusted to own

**Must answer:** Which outcomes or areas belong to this person? Which decisions can they make? What must they deliver themselves? Where do they coordinate, recommend, or escalate instead of deciding?

**Write:** five to nine ownership areas. Each gets a short action-led title and one paragraph that follows the same pattern:
1. The outcome or decision owned
2. A concrete example of the work
3. The boundary, collaborator, or escalation point, when it matters

**Quality test:** each item is a different area of accountability. The reader can picture making decisions, not just finishing tasks.

**Avoid:** minimum requirements mixed into responsibilities. Every task inflated into strategy. Software lists (unless a tool really defines the work). More than nine items without grouping.

## 4 What the work feels like

**Must answer:** How does the role split time and attention? What is recurring, reactive, collaborative, analytical, or creative? What might a typical week hold? Which tensions make it interesting?

**Write:**
- Three or four operating modes
- Five to seven examples for "a week in the seat" (real rhythms, not a made-up Monday-to-Friday calendar unless the schedule really is fixed)
- Two or three challenges worth solving

**Quality test:** the reader gets the texture of the work, not the responsibilities list again.

**Avoid:** repeating every responsibility. Pretending every week is the same. Calling ordinary work "high impact". Treating constant urgency as culture.

## 5 Company and business context

**Must answer:** What does the company or group do? Who does it serve? What business model or operating reality shapes this role? Why does that matter for the candidate's work?

**Write:**
- A role-relevant heading
- Two or three short paragraphs, opening with one plain sentence of what the company does and for whom
- Three or four useful facts or metrics (with dates or context where they age)

See the company through the lens of the role. A finance candidate needs business-model complexity. An engineer needs architecture and user conditions. A designer needs workflow and customer context.

**Quality test:** every company detail helps the candidate judge or understand the role.

**Avoid:** copying the About page. Unsupported growth or market claims. Old metrics with no date. Facts that are there only to impress.

## 6 Experience that will help you succeed

**Must answer:** What evidence should show up on a resume or LinkedIn? What deeper capability will interviews explore? What is truly required? What only speeds up ramp? Who is unlikely to enjoy or do well in this environment?

**Write:**
- A short intro stating the standard of evidence
- Five to nine core requirements, each a bold label plus the observable evidence
- Three to six helpful but optional advantages
- A short "what we'll explore in interviews" paragraph
- Three to five honest not-fit statements about the work and conditions

Each requirement must work as a filter on its own. Cut any that follow automatically from another.

**Quality test:** a strong candidate can assess themselves, and a recruiter can apply the requirements consistently.

**Avoid:** personality adjectives presented as qualifications ("detail-oriented", "proactive"). Responsibilities repeated word for word. A degree requirement without a real reason. Years as the only sign of seniority. A not-fit list that sounds insulting or defensive.

Good: "You've personally owned a monthly close across multiple entities." Weak: "You're detail-oriented."

## 7 Success measures

**Must answer:** What should they learn first? What should they own on their own after onboarding? What changes should be visible because they joined? How will the company know the role is working?

**Write:**
- First 30 days, by 60 days, by 90 days
- Six- or twelve-month outcomes only when the role supports them
- One lasting result that explains the role's long-term value

Each outcome gets a short result-led title, the visible change, and enough context to tell progress from activity. If timing isn't validated, open the section with a line saying the milestones are proposed and will be adjusted to the starting condition.

**Quality test:** ambitious but achievable within the person's authority and resources.

**Avoid:** promising results mostly controlled by other teams. Onboarding activities presented as long-term success. Exact metrics the company hasn't validated. The responsibilities list rewritten in the future tense.

## 8 Who you'll work with

**Must answer:** Who is the manager? Which teams or outside partners matter most? What does good collaboration look like with each? What support, context, feedback, or access will the candidate get?

**Write:** three to five groups or named roles, one short paragraph each. Include the manager's working style when known and appropriate to share.

**Quality test:** it shows the human working environment, not only the org chart.

**Avoid:** anonymous labels when the name and title can be shared. Describing collaboration only as what others need from the candidate. Inventing a management or feedback cadence.

## 9 Hiring process

**Must answer:** How many stages? Who's involved? What does each stage evaluate? Is there an exercise? How long will it take? What should the candidate prepare?

**Write:**
- A numbered process, one distinct purpose per step, realistic durations when known
- A separate "in the first conversation" subsection: likely topics and what to have ready
- For any assessment: its purpose, rough time, whether there's prep, privacy (no confidential employer records), and how it's evaluated

**Quality test:** no mystery steps, and the candidate knows what preparation is useful.

**Avoid:** hidden tests. Framing attention to detail as a trap. The same interview purpose across several stages. Calling the process fast without a real timeframe.

## 10 Candidate FAQs

**Must answer:** real open questions about why the role exists, reporting line and authority, hands-on vs. strategic balance, team and people management, remote work and time-zone overlap, contractor or employment terms, pay and payment, tools and equipment, growth and performance reviews, travel, and assessments. Cover only the ones the page hasn't already answered clearly.

**Write:** eight to twelve questions in the candidate's own words, with direct answers (usually two to five sentences). Be honest about limits when a decision isn't final.

**Quality test:** every FAQ answers something the main page doesn't.

**Avoid:** marketing points repeated as FAQs. Dodging hard questions. Hiding important terms only inside an accordion. Questions no candidate would actually ask.

## 11 Further reading

**Must answer:** Which outside sources help the candidate understand the company, product, customers, founder, or market, and why is each worth their time?

**Write:** three primary resources, plus up to three optional ones that add something different. Each gets a title, content type, one sentence on why it's worth reading, and a working direct link.

Priority: company or product site → product docs or demo → founder or leadership interview → independent customer reviews or industry coverage → company LinkedIn.

**Quality test:** it feels curated, not like a bibliography.

**Avoid:** more than six visible resources. Several links saying the same thing. Links without a reason. Company-produced content presented as independent evidence. Invented or unchecked URLs presented as checked.

## 12 Application close

**Must answer:** What should the candidate do next and send? What would make the application stronger? What happens after they apply?

**Write:**
- One specific closing heading (tied to the craft, e.g. "Show us what you've built in finance")
- A short instruction on what to send
- One primary action with the application link or email
- A short, respectful note on what happens next (reply timing, confidentiality)

**Quality test:** the candidate knows exactly what to submit and faces one clear action.

**Avoid:** "Join our team" style endings. A long cover letter with no stated purpose. More than one primary action. Re-pitching the whole role.
